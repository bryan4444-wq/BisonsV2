Oklahoma City's $123 Million Spending Spree: Inside the Bisons' Relentless Pursuit of a Contender

By Daniel Mercer | ESPN Senior NFL Reporter · February 25, 2027

Full signing and contract table · Bijan Robinson trade report

OKLAHOMA CITY — Three months ago, Bryan Luong was the NFL's youngest controlling owner, preparing to move a historic Indianapolis franchise to a city that had never hosted an NFL team.

Today, the 28-year-old billionaire has assembled one of the league's most intriguing rosters.

And he has done it with remarkable speed.

In a span of approximately 48 hours, Oklahoma City's Bisons finalized a blockbuster trade for superstar running back Bijan Robinson and confirmed 14 free-agent signings worth approximately $123.41 million in combined average annual salaries.

The spending spree includes a new defensive front, a reconstructed secondary, additional receiving weapons for Justin Herbert, and enough tight ends to make opposing defensive coordinators reconsider their personnel packages.

The most remarkable part?

Oklahoma City still reported approximately $113 million in available salary-cap space on its latest Madden financial screen.

The Bisons aren't simply spending. They're attempting to build an entire championship-caliber roster in one offseason.

The $197 Million centerpiece

The defining transaction came Wednesday when Oklahoma City completed its acquisition of 25-year-old running back Bijan Robinson from the Atlanta Falcons.

The price was substantial.

For Oklahoma City, the trade represents an unmistakable philosophical shift.

Hampton was supposed to be a foundational piece of the franchise's future. Aiyuk had barely received an opportunity to establish chemistry with Herbert and Marvin Harrison Jr.

Both were sacrificed for one extraordinary offensive talent.

Robinson now joins Herbert, Harrison, and second-year tight end Tyler Warren in an offense with the potential to threaten defenses at every level.

But there is considerable risk.

The Bisons surrendered two established offensive players and valuable draft capital to acquire a running back at a position where longevity and financial value remain persistent league-wide concerns.

If Robinson delivers elite production, the deal could accelerate Oklahoma City's competitive timeline.

If he doesn't, the price will follow Luong for years.

Buckner's arrival gives Oklahoma City an accomplished veteran interior defender. Diaby adds an established edge presence opposite Laiatu Latu, while Holland brings additional experience to a young secondary.

Williams, meanwhile, could be among the more intriguing value acquisitions of the offseason.

At 33, he isn't a long-term solution. But a defensive tackle with his experience at less than $7 million annually offers substantial rotational flexibility.

There is one unresolved question.

Can defensive coordinator Lou Anarumo maximize this personnel in Oklahoma City's planned 4–3 alignment?

The organization has already committed to changing its defensive structure, but its coordinator's long-term fit remains unsettled.

An abundance of talent does not automatically produce an effective defense.

Someone still has to make the pieces work together.

Herbert finally gets his supporting cast

Oklahoma City's offensive approach has been considerably more economical outside its headline trade.

Luong declined to pursue an approximately $30 million-per-year commitment to wide receiver George Pickens, instead targeting complementary weapons.

The result is an unusually deep collection of receivers, running backs and tight ends.

Bates, originally a tight end, has already appeared at fullback on Oklahoma City's latest depth chart.

The conversion gives head coach Shane Steichen another potential blocking option in front of Robinson.

Downs' return is particularly notable. The receiver previously departed in the 2026 Herbert transaction before returning through free agency. Questions surrounding his health remain, however, and his availability has not been conclusively established.

The organization also signed running backs Chase Brown and Sean Tucker, along with veteran backup quarterback Drew Lock.

For Steichen, the challenge is no longer identifying enough offensive talent.

It's deciding how to distribute touches.

With Harrison, Dell, Downs, Warren, Kmet, Hockenson and Robinson all requiring attention, there are only so many footballs to go around.

The offensive line remains a work in progress

Despite Oklahoma City's aggressive spending, Luong and his football operations staff have resisted making another expensive offensive-line acquisition.

Instead, the Bisons are exploring a competition-oriented approach.

Young guards Febechi Nwaiwu and Jalen Farmer could compete for starting opportunities, with Cole Strange providing another option.

Mike Onwenu, currently listed at right guard, is being considered for a move back to right tackle, where he would compete with Matt Goncalves.

The idea carries obvious appeal: develop younger players while maximizing the versatility of existing veterans.

But protecting Herbert and opening lanes for Robinson will be essential.

An expensive backfield means little if the offensive line cannot consistently create space.

The figures illustrate why Oklahoma City's spending spree is so unusual.

Most expansion-style roster rebuilds involve several years of accumulating draft picks, developing young talent and selectively pursuing veterans.

The Bisons have chosen a more aggressive path.

Their ownership group has already committed billions to acquiring and relocating the franchise, while Luong's football department has repeatedly demonstrated a willingness to move significant assets for established talent.

Still, the club has not abandoned its future entirely.

Oklahoma City retains the No. 13 overall selection in the upcoming draft, providing another opportunity to add an impact player on a rookie contract.

The question nobody can answer yet

Luong's first season as an NFL owner ended with a 9–8 record, following an extraordinary organizational transition that began in Indianapolis and concluded with the franchise's farewell to its longtime home.

The Bisons have yet to play their first regular-season game representing Oklahoma City.

That makes the expectations surrounding this offseason both fascinating and dangerous.

This is not a conventional rebuild. Nor is it a roster being assembled patiently around a single young quarterback.

It is an aggressive attempt to compress years of team-building into months.

Herbert provides the quarterback foundation. Robinson offers elite offensive versatility. Harrison remains a young receiving centerpiece. Buckner, Holland and Diaby bring experience and talent to a defense still establishing its identity.

And overseeing everything is a 28-year-old owner who has demonstrated little hesitation about making enormous decisions.

The remaining questions are substantial.

Can the offensive line develop quickly enough? Can the defense transition successfully to its new structure? Can Steichen manage a crowded collection of offensive weapons? And will the franchise's aggressive spending translate into wins?

Oklahoma City's front office has answered nearly every personnel question with another acquisition.

Eventually, the answers must come on the field.

For now, one thing is certain: the Bisons have ensured that the NFL will be watching Oklahoma City long before the opening kickoff.
